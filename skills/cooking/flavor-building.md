# Flavor Building & Recipe Logic

Great cooking is reproducible once the underlying logic is understood. Most recipes are applications of a small number of techniques to an ingredient matrix. Understanding the logic allows improvisation, substitution, and rescuing a dish that isn't working.

## The Four Elements (Nosrat)

Every balanced dish balances four dimensions:

| Element | Role | Signs of imbalance |
|---------|------|--------------------|
| **Salt** | Amplifies all other flavors; suppresses bitterness | Under-salted: flat, muted; over-salted: one-note, harsh |
| **Fat** | Carries fat-soluble flavor; texture; richness | Too little: dry, thin flavor; too much: heavy, coating |
| **Acid** | Brightens; cuts richness; contrast | Missing acid: heavy, cloying; too much: sharp, sour |
| **Heat** | Transforms raw ingredients; texture and color via Maillard/caramelization | Wrong temperature: raw centers, burnt exteriors, texture failure |

When a dish tastes "off," diagnose by element: flat → try salt or acid. Harsh → reduce acid or heat. Heavy → add acid. Flavorless despite seasoning → fat missing.

## Maillard Reaction vs. Caramelization

Both create brown color and complex flavor — the mechanisms differ:

- **Maillard reaction:** amino acids + sugars, occurs 140–165°C. Responsible for browned meat, bread crust, roasted coffee, sautéed onions. Requires a dry surface (wet = steaming, not browning).
- **Caramelization:** sugars alone, occurs 160–180°C. Caramelized onions, crème brûlée, tarte tatin.

**Practical implication:** pat meat dry before searing. Don't crowd the pan (steam lowers surface temp). High heat, brief contact for sear; lower heat for internal doneness.

## Building Flavor in Layers

The sequence of adding ingredients matters because each step builds on the last:

1. **Aromatic base** (mirepoix, soffritto, mirepoix, mirepoix) — sweat aromatics (onion, garlic, celery, carrot) in fat to develop foundation flavor
2. **Spices and dry ingredients** — bloom in fat or toast dry; heat unlocks volatile compounds
3. **Deglaze** — liquid lifts the fond (browned bits) from the pan; this is concentrated flavor
4. **Liquid phase** — stock, wine, tomato; simmer rather than boil for cleaner flavor
5. **Finish** — fresh herbs, acid, fat, salt adjust at the end; heat destroys delicate aromatics

## Substitution Logic

When an ingredient is missing, find its *function*, not its name:

| Function | Ingredient | Substitutes |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| Acid | Lemon juice | Lime, white wine vinegar, rice vinegar |
| Richness/fat | Butter | Olive oil, coconut cream, cream |
| Umami depth | Parmesan | Miso, soy sauce, fish sauce (small amount), nutritional yeast |
| Aromatic base | Yellow onion | Shallot, leek, fennel base |
| Binding | Egg | Flax egg, aquafaba, mashed banana (sweet) |

The more similar the substitution in the key function (not just flavor), the more successful the swap.

## Taste vs. Flavor

- **Taste:** detected by tongue only — sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami
- **Flavor:** taste + retronasal aroma (what you smell as you chew) + texture, temperature, pain (capsaicin)

This is why food tastes blander with a blocked nose. It also explains why wine pairing, texture contrast, and temperature matter so much to the experience.

## Recipe Reading Skills

Before starting any recipe:
1. **Read the entire recipe** — discover timing conflicts or parallel steps
2. **Identify the technique** — identify what's actually being done (braising, not just "cook")
3. **Note technique-critical details** — "reduce to a simmer" vs. "maintain a rolling boil" are opposite states

Recipes describe execution of a technique applied to specific ingredients. Understanding the technique lets you rescue deviations; following steps blindly does not.
