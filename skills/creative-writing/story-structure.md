# Story Structure & Scene-Level Craft

Structure is not a formula — it's a map of reader expectation. Audiences internalize story rhythm through thousands of hours of stories; violating that rhythm produces confusion or disengagement. Structure gives writers a shared language to diagnose why something isn't working.

## Three-Act Structure

| Act | Proportion | Function |
|-----|-----------|---------|
| Act 1 — Setup | ~25% | Establish world, protagonist, stakes; end with the inciting incident / turning point into Act 2 |
| Act 2 — Confrontation | ~50% | Rising conflict; midpoint shift; progressive complications; end with crisis (all seems lost) |
| Act 3 — Resolution | ~25% | Climax where protagonist must act on their transformation; resolution of central question |

The midpoint is often underestimated: it's where the protagonist's goal shifts from reactive to active, or where a major revelation reframes the story's stakes.

## Scene-Level Structure (Swain's Scene/Sequel)

Every scene (where action happens) ideally has:
1. **Goal** — what the POV character wants in this scene
2. **Conflict** — obstacle preventing immediate achievement
3. **Disaster** (or outcome) — the goal is not achieved cleanly; something changes

Every sequel (where the character processes and decides) has:
1. **Reaction** — emotional response to the disaster
2. **Dilemma** — no good options; a genuine choice with costs
3. **Decision** — the choice that drives the next scene's goal

Scene/sequel alternation creates the rhythm that makes readers turn pages. Pure action with no processing is exhausting; pure processing with no action is static.

## Character Arc

The external plot and the internal arc should be causally linked — what the character *does* in the external plot should arise from who they *are* and force who they *become*.

Three arc types:
- **Positive arc:** character moves from flawed belief → truth, enabled by the plot
- **Negative arc:** character doubles down on flawed belief, destroyed by it (tragedy)
- **Flat arc:** character's truth is tested but holds; their truth changes the world around them

Common beginner mistake: the external plot is fully developed but the internal arc is absent or decorative. The agent should always ask: what does this character believe at the start that is false, and what does the story cost them to discover?

## The Five "Show Don't Tell" Tools

"Show don't tell" is often misunderstood as "no emotions on the page." It means: *demonstrate* internal state through behavior, sensation, dialogue, and physical environment rather than summary label.

| Don't | Do |
|-------|----|
| She was nervous | She checked her phone three times in one minute |
| The room felt threatening | The overhead bulb buzzed; one chair faced the door |
| He loved her | He noticed when she changed her haircut. He said nothing. |

The exception: telling is efficient for low-stakes information. Show what matters; tell what contextualizes.

## Dialogue

Dialogue should do at least two of: advance plot, reveal character, establish relationship dynamics, create conflict, subtext. Dialogue that exists only to deliver exposition ("As you know, Bob...") is a red flag.

Subtext: what characters mean vs. what they say. Great dialogue lives in the gap.

## Diagnosing Common Problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| "Nothing happens" | Scenes have no goal or conflict | Apply scene structure; every scene must change something |
| Characters feel flat | Absent internal arc; no contradiction | Add a core false belief; let behavior reveal it |
| Pacing feels off | Scene/sequel imbalance | Too much action = add sequel; too much processing = add scene goals |
| Opening feels slow | Setup before inciting incident | Push the inciting incident earlier; weave setup through it |
| Ending feels unearned | Climax doesn't require the protagonist's specific growth | Make the climax solvable only by the person the protagonist became |
