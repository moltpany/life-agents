# Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) Framework

MBSR is an 8-week structured program developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn at UMass Medical Center. Its core claim: systematic attention training reduces the stress reactivity that underlies anxiety, chronic pain, and emotional dysregulation. The mechanism is *noticing* — observing thoughts, sensations, and emotions as transient events rather than facts.

## The three modes of practice

| Mode | Type | Example |
|------|------|---------|
| **Formal practice** | Dedicated, scheduled sessions | Body scan, sitting meditation, mindful movement |
| **Informal practice** | Bringing awareness to routine activity | Eating, walking, washing dishes with full attention |
| **Inquiry** | Reflective dialogue on what arose in practice | "What did you notice? Where did the mind go?" |

A mindfulness session combines at least one formal practice with brief inquiry. Informal practice fills the gaps between sessions.

## Core formal practices

### Body Scan (20–45 min, beginner-friendly)
Systematic attention through the body from feet to head. Not relaxation — it's attention training that happens to induce relaxation. Instructions:
1. Lie down, eyes closed
2. Focus on the breath at the belly for 2–3 breaths to settle
3. Move attention slowly upward through each body region; notice sensation (tingling, pressure, warmth, absence of sensation) without judgment
4. If the mind wanders (it will), notice, name it ("thinking"), and return to the body region
5. End with awareness of the body as a whole

The body scan is often the entry point — accessible because attention has a clear anchor.

### Mindful Breathing (5–20 min)
Attend to the physical sensations of breath: at the nostrils, chest, or belly. Not control of breath — observation of it.

A useful anchor instruction: "Breathing in, I know I am breathing in. Breathing out, I know I am breathing out."

When the mind wanders: notice, gently label ("planning," "worrying," "remembering"), return without judgment. Wandering is not failure — the returning IS the practice.

### Sitting Meditation (20–45 min)
Expands the anchor from breath to open awareness: sounds, sensations, thoughts, and emotions, attended to as they arise and pass. The progression in MBSR: begin with breath → expand to body → expand to sounds → open monitoring of whatever arises.

### Mindful Movement (10–30 min)
Gentle yoga or mindful walking — the same quality of attention applied to movement. Useful for people who find seated practice difficult.

## MBSR 8-week arc (for reference)

| Week | Focus |
|------|-------|
| 1 | Automatic pilot: noticing how we do things habitually |
| 2 | Dealing with barriers: first encounter with wandering mind |
| 3 | Mindfulness in daily life: informal practice introduction |
| 4 | Stress reactivity: the stress response and the pause between stimulus and response |
| 5 | Responding vs. reacting: allowing and turning toward difficulty |
| 6 | Thoughts as thoughts: cognitive defusion, not cognitive restructuring |
| 7 | Taking care of yourself: what nourishes and depletes |
| 8 | Using what has been learned: sustaining practice after the course |

An agent doesn't run the full program, but understanding the arc allows session pacing and progression.

## Session facilitation principles

1. **Invite, don't prescribe.** "You might notice..." not "You will feel..."
2. **Normalize wandering.** The mind's wandering is the curriculum, not the problem.
3. **Inquiry over instruction.** After a practice: "What did you notice?" before explaining what *should* have happened.
4. **Dose appropriately.** Start with 5–10 minute practices; build to 20–45 min. A short practice done consistently beats a long one abandoned.
5. **Distinguish discomfort from distress.** Some discomfort (boredom, restlessness) is productive and workable. Significant distress, especially trauma-related responses, warrants discontinuing and, if persistent, referral.

## Scope boundary

Mindfulness practice is not therapy. Do not position it as treatment for diagnosed clinical conditions (PTSD, clinical depression, panic disorder). For users describing symptoms that sound clinical, recommend professional support alongside or before starting a practice. A mindfulness agent facilitates practice — it does not assess or treat.
