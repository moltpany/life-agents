# Itinerary Design Principles

A good itinerary matches activity density to traveler energy, minimizes logistical friction, and places the right experiences at the right moments. The enemy of a great trip is an over-packed itinerary that turns exploration into a checklist.

## Pacing Model

### Activity density per day

| Trip type | Max scheduled activities | Principle |
|-----------|--------------------------|-----------|
| City break (2–4 days) | 3–4 distinct activities | High energy, compressed time |
| Extended holiday (7–14 days) | 2–3 per day | Sustainable; allow drift |
| Long journey (2+ weeks) | 1–2 per day | Pace for the full duration |

One "activity" = one museum, neighborhood, day trip, or experience of ~2–3 hours. Underestimate consistently: travel takes longer than Google Maps suggests.

### Buffer principles

- **30-minute buffers** between back-to-back activities
- **One free afternoon per 3 days** — serendipity needs space; the unplanned becomes the memorable
- **Travel day ≠ activity day** — arrival and departure days should have minimal or zero scheduled activities
- **Jet lag allowance:** 1 recovery day for every 3 time zones crossed, at the start of the trip

## Geographic Logic

Cluster activities by neighborhood or zone to minimize transit overhead. A common beginner mistake is scheduling sites by interest category (all museums first, then markets) rather than by location — this creates exhausting daily cross-city movements.

Build itineraries spatially: draw the trip on a map before adding to a calendar. Identify natural zones and schedule each zone together.

For multi-city trips: route by geography, not by ranking. Flying A→C→B→D because C and B are "better" wastes days to transit.

## Interest Matching

Before building an itinerary, establish the traveler's type across three axes:

| Axis | Spectrum |
|------|----------|
| Pace | Explorer (high density, many places) ↔ Dweller (few places, deep) |
| Texture | Urban (art, food, architecture, nightlife) ↔ Natural (landscapes, hiking, wildlife) |
| Engagement | Active (hiking, cycling, cooking classes) ↔ Receptive (museums, performances, beaches) |

Combine the traveler's profile with the destination's strengths. A "Dweller / Natural / Active" traveler in Tokyo needs a very different plan than a city-break classic.

## Peak-End Rule (Kahneman)

People remember experiences by their most intense moment (the peak) and their final impression (the end). Implications:

- **Plan your peak deliberately:** what is the single best experience this trip can offer? Schedule it for peak energy (mid-trip, not on arrival day or the day before departure).
- **End on a high:** the final day or evening should be pleasant and unhurried, not logistically stressful (late flights, early checkouts, heavy transit).
- **Endings matter more than middles:** a bad final day can shade the memory of an excellent week.

## Budget Distribution

As a rough guide for leisure travel:

| Category | Typical range (% of total) |
|----------|-----------------------------|
| Accommodation | 35–45% |
| Transport (flights + local) | 25–35% |
| Food | 15–20% |
| Activities & experiences | 10–15% |
| Buffer / contingency | 10% |

Adjusting: prioritize accommodation if rest quality matters (families, longer trips); prioritize experiences for short trips or adventure travel. A bad hotel affects every day; a bad restaurant affects one meal.

## Practical Checklist

Before finalizing any itinerary:
- [ ] All booking deadlines noted (popular sites, popular restaurants often require advance booking)
- [ ] Transit times verified, not estimated
- [ ] Accommodation check-in/check-out times noted
- [ ] One free half-day per 3 days built in
- [ ] Peak experience identified and protected
- [ ] Final day relaxed enough to end well
